Despite how inexhaustible these forests appeared to be, this trend continued for the next few decades: Californians' demand for timber vastly exceeded the rate of its recovery. By the 1880s, "no unlogged stands [of trees] remained south or east of San Francisco..."[^1] Several decades after the Gold Rush, Californians exhausted more and more redwood forests to construct Santa Clara, Santa Cruz, San Jose, and many other surrounding communities. Their timber frenzy left behinds fields of stumps where forests once stood.

Seen in the lithograph above, part of this timber frenzy included leisure and cultural exhibitions. The top image depicts wealthy Californians using the famous stump of the "Discovery Tree" as a dance floor, found in the Calaveras Grove. Said tree was felled in the Mid-19th Century and used for tourism and such displays because of its sheer size and novelty, much to the dismay of environmentalists. The bottom images show the "General Noble Tree," which was felled in the early 1890s to be displayed at the 1893 World Columbian Exposition in Chicago. With the stump measuring at almost 30ft in diameter, this ancient tree was over 300ft tall before it was felled explicitly for display in Chicago.

[^1]: Susan R. Schrepfer, _The Fight to Save the Redwoods: A History of Environmental Reform, 1917-1978_ (Madison: _University of Wisconsin Press_, 1983), 7.

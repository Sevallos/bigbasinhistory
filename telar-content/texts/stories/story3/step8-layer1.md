title: Governor Hiram Johnson and Automobiles
---

<img width="323" height="356" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/f7100423-5e35-46f8-9c91-ccceb3d3d86f" /> _Los Angeles Herald, October 21, 1916._ Accessed via University of California Riverside: California Digital Newspaper Collection.

The rise of automobiles and the 1910 Election of Governor Hiram Johnson in California further weakened the monopoly railroad companies had on travel. Johnson boldly proclaimed that his election campaign represented "a contest for freedom from the Southern Pacific, which has debauched, polluted, and corrupted our state."[^1] He illustrated this by traveling throughout the state by automobile rather than by train, which became a staple of his political career. He placed the newly-arrived automobiles at the center of a class and political rejection of Southern Pacific and their infamous reputation for corruption and monopolies.

For the Sempervirens Club, Johnson's electoral victory marked a decisive turning point to gain political support for their Saratoga Road proposal. By 1912, Johnson threw his support behind the state park by serving as one of its park commissioners alongside representatives of Stanford and Santa Clara Universities. As a politician that promoted the value behind automobile travel as an alternative to trains, he aided the Sempervirens Club in passing legislation. When Robert E. Kenna passed away in 1912, Johnson wrote a tribute to him and his efforts, commending Kenna as a Californian and dedicated nature preservationist whose "final words included his plans for protection and preservation of the trees."[^2]

[^1]: John Mack Faragher, California: An American History (New Haven, CT: Yale University Press, 2022) 284.

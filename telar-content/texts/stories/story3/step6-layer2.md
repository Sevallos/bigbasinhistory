title: An Appreciation in Academia & in Creative Writing
---

The book described an early geological history of Big Basin (likely with the consultation of Stanford University's professors) as well as the Sempervirens Club history that led up to the park's preservation. Additionally, Taylor made sure to emphasize the scientific value of Big Basin's preservation by featuring the academic writing of Dr. Isabel McCracken of Stanford University. She and many other students of the natural sciences appreciated Big Basin for both its scientific and recreational uses, illustrating a connection between accessible public parks and academic research. In Isabel McCracken's passage, she encouraged readers to hike along Big Basin's trails by describing the many kinds of beautiful flowers and shrubs they could find among the redwoods:

> "Perhaps the choicest shrub of our mountain woods is the beautiful and fragrant azalea [Rhododendron occidentalis), with its fine, large clusters of handsome white blossoms, blotched with yellow and sometimes shaded with pink. In the park we find it at its very best. It follows the numerous brooks and creeks that find their way through the Basin, gracefully throwing its branches out over the quiet pools, where the fishes dart, and over the tumbling rapids where the water ousels sport. Its charming clusters of blossoms and leaves of a rare, fresh green may be seen on all sides throughout the early summer months, but nowhere in greater profusion than out on Trail Beautiful, well on toward the China Grade turn-off."[^1]

Several chapters feature the creative and nature writing of Virginia Garland, a local naturalist and writer to the Santa Cruz Mountains. She was known for her extensive knowledge of plants and wildlife, so Taylor included her work in order to further illustrate the value and scenic beauty of Big basin. In the passage below, she described the value in identifying the individual bird species among Big Basin and the beauty of sharing said knowledge among loved ones and friends:
> "If, when your visit to the park comes to a close, you have learned to identify these little brothers of the air, school-teacher, parent, laborer, or professional man or woman, you will carry away in your heart a lesson of infinite value. If you can say with authority, "There is Junco--see the white flit of his tail; there is the Spurred red-eyed Towhee. Hark, a Trasher is singing in the thicket," or if far away from the woods, you speak of the California Towhee, the White Crown and Golden Crown Sparrow, you will never find the words come amiss, no matter when, how, or where, you essay to use them."[^2]

[^1]: Isabel McCracken, “Flora of California Redwood Park,” in _California Redwood Park, Sometimes Called Sempervirens Park; An Appreciation_ (Sacramento: W.M. Richardson, 1912) 70.
[^2]: Virginia Garland, “Bird Life in the Park,” in _California Redwood Park, Sometimes Called Sempervirens Park; An Appreciation_ (Sacramento: W.M. Richardson, 1912) 103-104.

---
title: Sempervirens Assumptions About Pristine Wilderness
---

When the Sempervirens Club rallied to preserve Big Basin, they acted in opposition to lumber & milling companies, as well as the corporate interests of large stakeholders and railroad companies surrounding the Santa Cruz Mountains. By the time the club formed in May 1900, nearly 100% of the mountain range's redwood forests were privately owned. Because of this, the club held the impression that Big Basin had only been "discovered" by people around fifty to sixty years prior. They envisioned Big Basin as a pristine wilderness that thrived without human intervention for millennia until greedy woodsmen trampled upon its forests and threatened its most ancient redwoods.

The problem with this assumption is that even when the Sempervirens Club truly were ignorant as to who could have lived in Big Basin, a number of their written works still dehumanized the forest's past inhabitants. Additionally, by assuming that such lush environments existed because of a lack of human intervention, they along with many environmentalists dismissed Indigenous stewardship over the land. Such beliefs perpetuate the Vanishing Indian Myth that assumes the mere "disappearance" of Indigenous peoples, rather than their systemic removal and genocide having taken place since Spanish Missionization and that accelerated after the American Gold Rush. Written prose like below aided in perpetuating this myth by comparing Indigenous peoples to the Pacific madrone trees found at the park:
> "Seen through the tangle of the thicket, it looks like the brown, lithe body of an Indian, and in the moonlight the graceful upsweep of its branches like the careless lifting of a dusky maiden’s arms."[^1]
> "the madronas (the strawberry trees [sic], the real red Indians of the California forest."[^2]

[^1]: Arthur A. Taylor, 57.
[^2]: Arthur A. Taylor, 56.

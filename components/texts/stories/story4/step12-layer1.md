Hill's house remains preserved today in San Jose's History Park, just down the street from Andrew P. Hill High School. Placards, docents, and public memory generally dub Hill as "The Man who Saved the Redwoods," acknowledging his key role in sparking the movement that established the park. Later club president Herbert C. Jones (between 1945 and 1968) compared Hill's leadership to Lincoln with his generals: "Lincoln had generals, and Hill had his generals and lieutenants in the project." While popular history tends to promote the idea of "great men in history," the more remarkable fact about Hill is that he was a man of average means. Other state & national park histories center around famous writers/artists and wealthy philantrophists, but Hill was neither famous nor wealthy, and he would remain as such even as club president up to his passing in 1922.

Hill saw himself as a man of his community. His photography was his livelihood, and through his day-to-day work, he established connections with local publications, businesses, and educators. When the Sempervirens Club hosted a celebration in 1913 for their efforts and recent successful road campaign, Hill was hesitant to accept credit as its leader even as club president. He credited Reverend Robert E. Kenna, Professor William R. Dudley, Josephine McCrackin, and the many members and supporters of the club, stating: “this has not been a one-man’s fight nor a one-woman’s fight. It has been a fight of organizations of the people of California… [working] like a team."[^1]

[^1]: DeVries, Grand and Ancient Forest: The Story of Andrew P. Hill and Big Basin Redwoods State Park (Fresno, CA: Valley Publishers, 1978) 59.

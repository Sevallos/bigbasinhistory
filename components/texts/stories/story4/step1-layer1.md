---
title: Environmental History & the Sublime
---

When discussing the early history of the national parks, environmental historian William Cronon concluded that feelings of the sublime dominated American attitudes toward wilderness, especially the unique and/or dramatic landscape of the American West. Massive canyons at Grand Canyon and Yellowstone, high mountaintops, and giant sequoias and redwoods over a thousand years old evoked a "feeling [of being] insignificant and being reminded of one's own mortality."[^1] 

Literature, photography and artwork played a significant role in early environmental activism in the United States, as they served as among the primary means of conveying to the public the significance of preserved wilderness. Entire debates over the definition and importance of wilderness (that continue today) often spurred out of such artistic portrayals of the environment since these depictions would be the vast majority of the public's first impression of these remote spaces. Religious connotations, scientific value, and comparisons to ancient wonders/monarchs dominated such narratives to convey to audiences that same sense of insignificance and the sublime.

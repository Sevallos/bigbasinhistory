---
title: "Half a Century of Redwood Destruction"
---

The story of redwood destruction did not begin in the Santa Cruz Mountains, but rather had been occurring throughout northern California since the Gold Rush. Around this time, Americans crossing into California witnessed redwoods and giant sequoias for the first time. Although many of them expressed their awe with the sheer size of these trees, they were swift to recognize the value of even a single tree for its timber. The abundance of these massive trees allured lumber mills and timber production companies with what appeared to be an inexhaustible supply.

The Gold Rush exacerbated the demand for timber. Within the first few years of the Gold Rush, tens of thousands of people from the United States and around the world ventured into California for gold and gold-adjacent opportunities. In less than ten years from the Gold Rush's start in 1849, San Francisco alone had ballooned from a population of 1000 to over 50,000. From this population surge, the California redwoods became not just valuable but vital for housing, fencing, agriculture, tools, mining, transportation, harbor construction, and so on. By the mid-1850s, San Francisco exhausted its immediate surrounding redwood forests.[^1]

[^1]: Jared Farmer, _Trees in Paradise: A California History_ (New York: _W.W. Norton & Company Inc_, 2013) 48.

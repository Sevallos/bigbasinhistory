---
title: "Nestled within the Santa Cruz Mountains"
---

Big Basin's remoteness spared its redwoods from lumbermen for a few decades after the Gold Rush. Though technically owned by private lumber interests as early as 1861, the difficult mountainous terrain made it impossible for redwood extraction and timber profits until the mid-1880s. By then, Southern Pacific Railroad extended its tracks through the Santa Cruz Mountains and effectively opened up its forests for many eager lumber companies. Up until the turn of the twentieth-century, they purchased and acquired the valuable redwood forests for timber profits.[^1]

![46888](https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/a2a1ed18-f844-4026-8b7d-138077db860d) "Hills Eastward Show the Total Denudation of Timber." Photograph by Andrew P. Hill, 1901.

Because Big Basin's remoteness delayed the lumbermen until the 1880s, Big Basin's redwoods were uniquely among the largest and oldest remaining trees near the San Francisco Bay Area. Other past redwood forests had been entirely depleted, with other remaining Old Growth forests located at least dozens of miles away in remote locations. Big Basin, in comparison, was relatively close to both urban and rural peoples between Santa Cruz, Santa Clara, and San Francisco. Early on in the Sempervirens Club's history, Big Basin's location made accessibility to nature a core motivation for preservation.

[^1]: Traci Bliss, _Big Basin Redwood Forest: California’s Oldest State Park_ (Charleston, SC: _The History Press_, 2021) 9.

---
title: An Automobile Trend that Spread to Other Parks Too
---

The railroad monopoly over nature recreation had previously dominated National Parks like Glacier and Yellowstone, but that monopoly swiftly dwindled following the rise of automobiles in the 1910s. Even though major railroad companies tried to lobby against and restrict car traffic into these parks, the popularity of cars ultimately triumphed over these policy proposals.[^1] By the 1910s, the Department of the Interior both permitted automobile traffic into the National Parks and funded road construction. The following decades, more state and federal entities (like the CCC during the New Deal Era) constructed more roads, parking lots, and campgrounds to accommodate visitors rather than hotels. The decades-long monopoly that the railroad industry had over nature tourism evaporated within less than a decade, as the car swiftly became the choice of transportation for the American middle class.

In California as well, a few years after the Sempervirens Club campaigned on automobile roads, so did the Save the Redwoods League when they famously advertised their cause with the scenic Redwood Highway.[^2] Debates over automobile accessibility into the parks continued well throughout the twentieth century, with some parks like Mount Rainier National Park embracing a scenic drive through the park, and others like North Cascades National Park largely restricting automobile access.[^3] In any case, the debate had shifted entirely away from the railroads, who were once seen as massive corporate entities in this country that held a monopoly over most major domestic industries.
